Short answer: yes, music helps language learning, and there's real research behind that. It helps most with three things: getting vocabulary to stay put, tuning your ear and your pronunciation, and giving you a reason to come back tomorrow.

It doesn't teach you grammar, and it won't get you through a conversation on its own. Anyone who tells you a playlist replaces a course is selling a playlist.

The rest of this post is the longer version: what the studies found, why it works when it works, and how to use songs so you're studying rather than just listening.

## What does the research actually say?

Start with the study most people are half remembering when they say "science says singing helps".

In 2014, researchers at the University of Edinburgh took sixty adults and gave them fifteen minutes to learn phrases in Hungarian, a language none of them knew. One group repeated the phrases by speaking them, one by speaking them rhythmically, one by singing them. [The singing group did best overall](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0342-5), and the gap was statistically significant on the two tests that mattered most: recalling the Hungarian phrases and saying them out loud. Age, mood, memory span and musical training didn't explain the difference.

An earlier experiment, published in *Cognition* in 2008, looked at something more basic. When you hear a language you don't know, you can't tell where one word stops and the next begins. It's all one stream. The researchers played people seven minutes of an invented language. [Spoken, seven minutes wasn't enough to start picking out the words. Sung, with each syllable on its own pitch, it was](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17475231/). The melody was doing part of the work of carving the stream into pieces.

And then a finding that explains a lot about why songs behave the way they do in your head. A 1994 memory study found that [text is recalled better as a song than as speech, but only when the melody repeats](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Memory-for-music:-Effect-of-melody-on-recall-of-Wallace/bbd36596cf39f88c1f3066526a96561b4768f484). Three verses on the same tune beat the spoken version. One verse, or a different tune for every verse, and the spoken version won. The tune has to become familiar before it starts carrying the words.

Now the part that usually gets left out. Not every study agrees. A 2007 experiment had people learn lines from unfamiliar folk songs, [sung or spoken, and singing gave no advantage at all](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193445). These are also small lab studies measuring minutes and days, not people reaching fluency over years.

So the fair summary is this. Music reliably helps you remember words and phrases under the right conditions, the main condition being repetition of a tune you know. Nobody has shown that it teaches you a language by itself.

## How does music help with learning new languages?

A few things are going on at once, and most of them aren't mysterious.

**Repetition you don't resent.** Vocabulary needs repetition, and repetition is boring, which is why word lists get abandoned. A chorus repeats the same lines three or four times per listen, and you'll replay a song you like fifty times without anyone asking you to. Nobody has ever reread a vocabulary list fifty times for fun.

**Built in structure.** Melody and rhythm cut a line into chunks, mark which syllables are stressed, and tell you how long the line is. That's scaffolding. When you forget a word in a lyric, the tune often hands it back to you, because you know a two syllable word with the stress on the first beat goes there.

**Real pronunciation.** Textbook audio is slow and careful. Songs show you how words run into each other in actual speech, which vowels get swallowed, and where the rhythm of the language sits. Your ear gets trained on the real thing.

**A reason to show up.** This one is less scientific and probably matters most. The best method is the one you still use in month four. Plenty of people quit language apps out of boredom rather than difficulty, and it's hard to be bored by a song you picked.

## Can you learn a language just by listening to music?

No, and you already have the proof. You can almost certainly sing along to a song in a language you don't speak. You've known the sounds for years. You still have no idea what it says.

That's what passive listening gets you: the sound without the meaning. It isn't worthless, since your ear is learning the rhythm and the sound system, but it isn't vocabulary.

Songs also have limits as study material even when you do the work:

- Lyrics bend grammar and word order to fit a rhyme, so don't treat a line as a model sentence.
- Singers stretch vowels and drop syllables in ways nobody does when talking.
- There's plenty of slang, and some of it is dated or regional.
- A song never asks you a question and waits for your answer. There's no speaking practice and no feedback.

So music is a strong second method and a poor only one. Pair it with something that explains grammar and something that makes you talk. If you're picking a course to go next to it, I compared the main options in our post on [Duolingo alternatives](/blog/duolingo-alternatives).

## Does singing help you learn a language, or is listening enough?

Singing is better, and the Edinburgh study is the reason to think so. Those learners didn't just hear sung phrases. They sang them back, and they were the ones who could produce the phrases afterwards.

That fits how memory generally works. Pulling a word out of your head and saying it is harder than recognising it, and the harder version is the one that builds the memory. Singing along also forces your mouth to make unfamiliar sounds at full speed, with the rhythm holding you to it.

You don't have to be any good. Car, shower, headphones and a closed door. The study found musical ability made no difference.

## How do you actually use songs to learn a language?

Here's the routine I'd suggest. It takes about fifteen minutes a song.

1. **Pick a song you like with clear vocals.** Slower is easier. Acoustic, pop and ballads beat anything where the voice is buried in the mix.
2. **Read the lyrics with a translation next to them** before you try to follow along. You want meaning attached to the sounds from the first listen.
3. **Listen while following the words.** Twice is plenty for the first sitting.
4. **Look up the words that repeat.** The chorus first. If a word shows up six times in three minutes, it's worth having.
5. **Sing along,** badly if necessary.
6. **Save five to ten words and review them over the next few weeks.** Spaced repetition, meaning reviews that arrive just before you'd forget, is what turns "I recognise that" into "I know that". Skip this step and most of the benefit leaks away.
7. **Come back to the song a week later.** Noticing how much more you understand is the best motivation you'll get all month.

## So is it a terrible idea to learn a language from songs?

As the whole plan, it's a weak one. You'd end up with good pronunciation, a strange vocabulary and no ability to order a coffee.

As part of a plan, it's one of the better ideas available. The research says sung words are easier to hold on to when the tune is familiar, that melody helps you hear where words begin and end, and that singing them yourself beats just listening. Common sense adds the rest: you'll do it more often than flashcards, because you like it.

Use a course for grammar. Find someone, or something, to talk to. And put the language in your ears in a form you'd choose anyway.
